Nine Days · Immersive · Culture · Weird

Tokyo. 深く.

One of the strangest cities on earth, if you go past the surface. The plan below is organized around neighborhoods, not monuments — each day drops you into a different version of Tokyo and leaves enough unscheduled time for the city to show you things that aren't in any guidebook.

Nine days

Each day is a neighborhood. Each neighborhood is a different city.

Day 1
Saturday, August 8 · Shinjuku · arrive, check in, rest

You land at 4:45pm Saturday — tired but not wrecked, and it's still daylight. Take the Narita Skyliner (41 min, ¥2,580) to Ueno, then the Yamanote to Shinjuku. You'll be at the hotel by about 6:30pm. Check in. Walk five minutes east into the neon of Kabukicho for dinner: ramen shops and small restaurants are everywhere, all good, all inexpensive. If it's before 22:00 and you have energy: Tokyo Mystery Circus escape room is 7 minutes from the Shinjuku East Exit — SCRAP's six-floor flagship. Book Escape from the Witch's House in the Woods at mysterycircus.jp/en if you want it (magic wand, talking portraits, 9 puzzle rooms — whimsical, not scary, 60 minutes). Otherwise: back to the hotel, sleep. The city will be here tomorrow.

Day 2
Sunday, August 9 · Asakusa & Akihabara · ancient and electric

At Senso-ji by 7am — Tokyo's oldest temple (628 AD), in the Asakusa district. Before the tour groups arrive, the incense smoke, the fortune slips, the 5-story pagoda, the old women sweeping the grounds — it's genuinely other-worldly. Walk Nakamise-dori for handmade crafts, then breakfast at a local kissaten. Mid-morning: a woodblock printing workshop at Mokuhankan immersive, the studio on the Asakusa backstreets run by Canadian master printer David Bull — one of the only places in the world still doing traditional ukiyo-e woodblock printing end-to-end. About a one-hour Print Party — you hand-print your own design from the master's pre-carved blocks (printing, not carving) and take it home. Book online before the trip. That's the anchor; Asakusa alone is a full morning with the jet lag still present. After lunch, if you have energy: subway to Akihabara weird — multi-story electronics and anime district, floors of used manga, figure stores. Any multi-floor gashapon hall weird sells capsule toys for ¥300–500 each — miniature food, surrealist objects, things you didn't know existed. A maid cafe weird if you're curious — servers in French maid costumes treating you as the returning master of the house; Kira Maid or Maidreamin are the most legible for first-timers. But if the jet lag is winning, stay in Asakusa, wander the backstreets, and call it a quiet afternoon. Seven more days follow.

Day 3
Monday, August 10 · teamLab + Shibuya · the most immersive day

Morning: teamLab Borderless immersive at Azabudai Hills (Roppongi area). Unlike Planets, there's no map, no fixed route, and no exits you're pushed toward — you wander between rooms that bleed into each other: forests of hanging lamps that respond to movement, crystal universes, gardens of floating flowers, spaces large enough to stay in for 30 minutes. Borderless rewards time; allow 2–3 hours and let it go where it goes. Book at teamlab.art 2–3 weeks ahead; time slots sell out. Azabudai Hills to Shibuya is ~5 minutes on the Hibiya line. Afternoon: Real Escape Game Crossing Shibuya escape room in Udagawacho — SCRAP's Shibuya venue with English-language rooms and themed narrative puzzles. One 60–90 minute session; book ahead at realescapegame.jp. Then walk the Shibuya Scramble from street level. A few minutes' walk away in Shibuya PARCO (6th floor): Nintendo TOKYO, Japan's first official Nintendo store — worth a browse. Then head to Shibuya Scramble Square and take the elevator to Shibuya Sky for the view from above — a 229m open-air rooftop deck with a glass-corner edge and cloud hammocks. As the sun goes down, the crossing turns into the most kinetic thing you've ever watched. Evening: into the Shibuya maze — Udagawacho record shops, late ramen, and whatever the neighborhood hands you.

Day 4
Tuesday, August 11 · Meiji, Harajuku & Kabuki · owls, ferrets, fashion, theater

Early at Meiji Jingu — a Shinto shrine inside a 170-acre forest in the center of the city. The approach through the torii gates and cedar trees is one of the more unexpectedly moving things in Tokyo. Then walk south into Harajuku for the animal cafe run — two consecutive stops on Takeshita Street: first an owl cafe weird (45 minutes with barn owls, great grey owls, tiny saw-whets perching on your arm and head), then Harajuku ZOO LAND weird (3F, 1-6-12 Jingumae — ferrets, hedgehogs, fennec foxes, and meerkats in one room, ¥1,400 adults, 40-minute session; book ahead on Klook). Then walk Takeshita-dori itself for the street fashion — decora, Lolita, cosplay, crepe stands — and slip into Ura-Harajuku (the back streets) for independent designers and vintage shops. Lunch in Omotesando. Afternoon train to Ginza: an act at Kabukiza Theater immersive. Single-act tickets (hitomaku-mi) go on sale at the box office the day of; no advance booking, about ¥1,000–2,000, earphone guides in English. You're watching a 400-year-old performance tradition: white-face makeup, male actors playing female roles, vocalizations that sound like nothing else. One act is about 45 minutes.

Day 5
Wednesday, August 12 · Yanaka + Ueno + Parasite Museum · old Tokyo, free museums, genuine dread

Yanaka in the morning — the neighborhood that survived WWII bombing intact, preserving old Tokyo: narrow lanes, independent shotengai shopping streets, Buddhist temples, cats on every corner. The cemetery here is famously beautiful, with cherry trees and winding paths. Walk through it. Continue to Ueno Park and the Tokyo National Museum — the most important collection of Japanese art and artifacts in the world, including swords, lacquerware, Buddhist sculpture, and Edo-period paintings. Allow at least two hours. Then: subway to Meguro for the Meguro Parasitological Museum weird. It's free, it takes 40 minutes, it contains over 300 preserved parasite specimens including a 29-foot tapeworm removed from a human. The gift shop sells parasite-themed merchandise. One of the strangest things you can do in any city on earth. Before the canal: Real Escape Game Crossing Shibuya escape room — pick a different room from Day 3 (Nine Rooms if you did Underwater Puzzle Challenge, or vice versa). Shibuya is 3 stops from Nakameguro on the Tokyu Toyoko line; one 60–90 minute room, then head east for the evening. Walk the Nakameguro canal, lined with independent restaurants and cafes.

Day 6
Thursday, August 13 · Tokyo DisneySea · the most elaborate theme park on Earth

30 minutes east to Urayasu: Tokyo DisneySea uniquely Japan — this park does not exist anywhere else on Earth. Eight nautically-themed ports, each with its own visual identity and story: Mediterranean Harbor (the entry, gorgeous Italian harbor setting), Mysterious Island (Jules Verne — Journey to the Center of the Earth + 20,000 Leagues Under the Sea), Mermaid Lagoon (The Little Mermaid — mostly indoor, a good afternoon heat refuge), Arabian Coast, Lost River Delta, American Waterfront, Port Discovery, and the newest: Fantasy Springs (2024 — Frozen, Rapunzel's forest, Peter Pan's Neverland). The theming depth is unlike anything in the US parks.

August strategy (this matters): Be at the gate before rope drop (~8am — verify your date). The moment you enter, purchase Premier Access (paid skip-the-line, similar to Lightning Lane) for Journey to the Center of the Earth and Tower of Terror. Do all big rides before 11am — by noon the heat and queues combine badly. After noon: shows, indoor theaters, the harbor boats, Fantasy Springs (it's mostly indoor). The evening water spectacular is worth staying for. Bring water. Wear light colors.

Day 7
Friday, August 14 · Kappabashi & Asakusa · fake food, real craft, slow day after Disney

The day after DisneySea — deliberately unhurried. Morning: Kappabashi weird — Tokyo's kitchenware and restaurant supply district (合羽橋), a long street of shops ending in the sampuru (食品サンプル) cluster at the south end. Sampuru are the hyper-realistic plastic food replicas in every Japanese restaurant window — and this is where they come from. You can buy individual pieces as souvenirs (a glistening slab of fake salmon, a bowl of ramen more convincing than the real thing, a wedge of impossibly layered cake) or do a sampuru workshop and make your own — typically a piece of tempura, dipping a wax-coated form and shaping it by hand. The workshop version is worth booking ahead; search "sampuru making workshop Kappabashi" for current operators. Kappabashi is 15 minutes on foot from Asakusa station. Afternoon: shibori indigo dyeing workshop immersive in the Asakusa or Yanaka area — traditional fabric dyeing, you leave with a scarf or cloth you made. Search "shibori workshop Asakusa" for current operators; book ahead. Easy dinner in the neighborhood.

Day 8
Saturday, August 15 · Ghibli + Shimokitazawa + Roppongi · Miyazaki, indie Tokyo, art Tokyo

Morning: Ghibli Museum uniquely Japan in Mitaka — assuming you landed tickets at the July 10 drop (see the Ghibli card and checklist). Take a 10:00 entry slot. Designed by Miyazaki himself: no fixed route, no signs, a rooftop robot soldier, the original short films screened only here. It's a half-day — allow ~2 hours inside plus the Inokashira Park walk to and from the station. The geography is the whole reason this lands on Day 8: the museum sits on the Keio Inokashira line, which runs straight down to Shimokitazawa (one line, no transfer, ~15 min). If you didn't get Ghibli tickets and skipped a tour: swap in Tokyo Mystery Circus escape room in Shinjuku instead (Escape from The Runaway Train, no horror), then head to Shimokitazawa. Either way: Shimokitazawa in the afternoon — the bohemian neighborhood: indie music venues (many legendary small live houses), vintage clothing stores so dense they've become an industry, kissaten with handwritten menus, small theaters. It looks nothing like Shinjuku or Harajuku. Walk without a map. Evening: cross-city to Roppongi for the art. The Mori Art Museum on the 53rd floor of Roppongi Hills is one of the best contemporary art museums in Asia — rotating major exhibitions and a permanent city view. One floor down (52F): the Tokyo City View observation deck, which at night is one of the most dramatic views in the city. Then: dinner in Roppongi, which has an outsized expat and international food scene relative to the rest of Tokyo.

Day 9
Sunday, August 16 · Tsukiji + fly home · breakfast at the market, then the long flight east

Tsukiji Outer Market by 8am: the best last breakfast in the city — grilled scallops, sea urchin on rice, tamagoyaki, fatty tuna. Caveat: it's Sunday, and many Tsukiji shops close Sundays — enough stalls stay open for a solid breakfast, but check the night before, or fall back to a breakfast near the apartment in Shinjuku if you'd rather not gamble on the departure morning. Back to the apartment by 11am — pack, check out (10am sharp), store bags at a coin locker at Shinjuku Station or nearby. Final wander. Narita Skyliner from Ueno — JL66 departs 17:15; aim to clear Narita security by 15:00, which means leaving Shinjuku by 13:00.

The genuinely weird — a shortlist

Things that have no equivalent anywhere else on earth.

SCRAP — the company that invented escape rooms ★

  • The escape room format was invented in Kyoto in 2007 by Takao Kato of SCRAP. Every escape room you've ever done traces back to this company and this city.
  • SCRAP's default style is puzzle/adventure, not horror — but they do make horror rooms, clearly labeled. Filter by theme when booking and avoid anything tagged "horror," "haunted," or "survival."
  • Rooms to avoid (jump scares / horror): Haunted Manor · Sadako and the Cursed Video · Survive the Urban Legend
  • Safe rooms on this plan: Witch's House in the Woods (magic wand + talking portraits, whimsical) · RED ROOM (pure logic, zero text) · Nine Rooms / Underwater Puzzle Challenge (Shibuya, puzzle chains) · Runaway Train (adventure tension, no horror) · Magic Academy Shinjuku (outdoor puzzle walk)

Ghibli Museum Day 8 · Mitaka / Kichijōji

  • Mitaka, on the Keio Inokashira line in Inokashira Park. Designed by Hayao Miyazaki himself — no signs, deliberately non-linear, meant to be explored not guided. There's no commentary inside; even paid tours just secure the ticket and walk you to the door.
  • ⏰ The play: be ready Thu July 9 at 6:00pm PDT (= Fri July 10, 10:00am JST). Tickets are not a lottery — they're a timed online release, first-come, opened at 10am JST on the 10th of the month for the following month, advance-only (nothing sold at the door). August tickets drop July 10 and sell out in minutes. Set an alarm.
  • How to be ready: buy through the official Lawson Ticket English site (l-tike.com) — create the account and save your card days before, and confirm the login works (the portal can be flaky from the US). At 10am JST sharp: target Sat Aug 15, 10:00 entry (Day 8). Backups if that slot is gone: Fri Aug 14, or any open morning Aug 9/12/14 — not Tue Aug 11 (closed Tuesdays). Have 2–3 date+slot combos ready. ID/passport required at entry; the purchaser must be in the group (no resale).
  • Guaranteed fallback if the drop fails: book a tour that pre-secures tickets — the Pelago half-day (guaranteed admission + Inokashira Park, ~3h, meet Kichijōji), the WILLER walking tour (tickets guaranteed for participants), a Viator private tour (verify in-browser — bot-blocked to link checkers), or the JTB/Sunrise bus tour (9h, ~¥26,000pp, adds the Edo-Tokyo Open Air Architectural Museum). These also sell out — if the July 10 self-buy misses, book a tour the same week, don't wait.
  • Either way, this is the Day 8 morning anchor — Kichijōji → Shimokitazawa is one line, ~15 min.

Don Quijote (Donki) at 2am

  • The discount store chain is open 24 hours. The Shinjuku or Shibuya branches are seven floors of organized chaos — food, wigs, cosplay costumes, electronics, toiletries, ceremonial items — all crammed with no apparent logic, jingle playing on loop.
  • Not a tourist attraction. Genuinely where Tokyoites shop at midnight. Go without an agenda and just wander.

Owl cafe ★ Day 4 · Harajuku

  • You sit in a room with free-roaming owls — barn owls, great grey owls, tiny saw-whet owls — that perch on your arm, your shoulder, wherever they feel like landing. Staff are present throughout.
  • Sessions typically 45–60 minutes, including a drink. Budget ¥2,000–3,800 — prices rose in 2026 (Owl Village near Takeshita-dori is now ¥3,800). Several operate in the Harajuku/Shibuya area; the ones near Takeshita-dori are easiest to stack with Day 4's itinerary.
  • Search "owl cafe Harajuku" for current operators — the field changes; a walk-in works but booking online removes uncertainty on a packed day.
  • August note: air-conditioned inside. Welcome.

Harajuku ZOO LAND ★ Day 4 · Takeshita Street · ferrets + exotics

  • 3F, 1-6-12 Jingumae, Shibuya — right on Takeshita Street, 3 minutes from the owl cafe.
  • Animals: ferrets (confirmed affectionate and very interactive), hedgehogs, fennec foxes, meerkats. All in one 40-minute session. (The capybara some guides mention is no longer reliably there — don't count on it.)
  • ¥1,400 adults · 1 drink included · Hours 11:00–19:00 daily (last entry 18:20)
  • Book ahead on Klook or KKday — Takeshita gets crowded in August and slots fill. Walk-ins possible but not guaranteed.
  • "Ferret Village" in Ikebukuro is not a verifiable operating venue — ZOO LAND is the confirmed pick.

2.5D Theater

  • Live musical theater adaptations of anime and manga, performed by human actors with elaborate staging. A major industry in Japan with dedicated theaters and fan communities.
  • Most shows are entirely in Japanese; no translation available. That's fine. The spectacle carries it.
  • Check what's playing at the Tokyo International Forum, TDC Hall, or AiiA Theater before you leave — if anything looks interesting, book it for Day 7 evening instead of Roppongi.

Kintsugi workshop

  • Kintsugi is the Japanese practice of repairing broken pottery with gold lacquer — the break becomes part of the beauty rather than a flaw to hide. The philosophy is as significant as the technique.
  • Several Tokyo studios offer 2–3 hour workshops: you bring or choose a broken ceramic piece, repair it with urushi lacquer and gold powder, take it home. Studios in Harajuku, Shimokitazawa, and Nakameguro areas offer sessions.
  • Book online before the trip — sessions fill quickly and most require advance registration. Search "kintsugi workshop Tokyo" for current offerings; the landscape changes seasonally.
  • For a Studio Art student: this is one of the most conceptually interesting craft experiences available in Japan. The aesthetics (wabi-sabi, mono no aware) are worth understanding before you go.

Ferret, hedgehog, and exotic animal cafes

  • Ferret cafe: Small rooms where ferrets roam freely — they're social and curious and will climb on you. Several in the Harajuku/Shibuya area.
  • Hedgehog cafe: Harry Harajuku and Mofuwa Hedgehog (Shibuya) are well-reviewed; you hold hedgehogs under staff supervision. 30–60 minute sessions.
  • Cat cafes are the most established; Temari no Ouchi (themed like a Victorian house) and Cat Cafe Mocha are popular. Most charge by the hour (¥800–1,200) plus a drink.
  • Owl cafes (covered in Day 4) are the most visually dramatic. Hedgehog cafes are the quietest. Ferret cafes are the most chaotic.

Book ahead for hedgehog and ferret cafes — slots are limited. Walk-ins work for most cat cafes.

Purikura photo booths

  • Purikura (print clubs) are photo booths that shoot you, then let you edit the image in real time — smooth skin, bigger eyes, add stickers, backgrounds, text, anything. The output is a sheet of tiny sticker-photos.
  • The machine's AI vision settings for "beauty" are aggressively calibrated for Japanese aesthetics. The results are startling. Arcades in Shibuya, Harajuku, and Ikebukuro all have floors of them.
  • Uniquely Japan in the most specific sense: this is a social ritual teenage girls have been doing for 30 years. The machines have evolved into something elaborate and strange.
  • ¥400–600 per session. Take 3–4 rounds to understand the editing interface.

Capsule hotel — one night

  • Not a budget move — the good ones (Nine Hours in Narita or Shinjuku, Book and Bed in Shinjuku) are thoughtfully designed and worth one night for the experience.
  • Book and Bed Shinjuku is specifically the one: a hotel where your sleeping unit is a shelf inside a large bookcase, surrounded by books. You read until you fall asleep.

Practical

Getting around

IC card (Suica or Pasmo) loaded at any station machine — covers subway, JR lines, buses, and many convenience store purchases. ¥5,000 to start. Never take taxis for cross-city travel; the subway is faster, cheaper, and runs until midnight. Taxis after midnight are fine and metered.

Google Maps works perfectly for Tokyo transit routing in English — enter a destination and it gives you the exact platform and exit number.

Money

Japan is still predominantly cash. Many restaurants, small shops, and temples don't take cards. Withdraw yen at 7-Eleven ATMs (most reliable for foreign cards) or Japan Post offices. ¥50,000 should cover a week of food and local purchases.

Tipping is not practiced and can cause confusion. Don't tip.

Language

English signage is excellent in stations and tourist areas. Google Translate camera mode handles menus (point at text, it translates in real time). Most restaurant counters have plastic food displays or picture menus. "Sumimasen" (excuse me) and "arigato gozaimasu" (thank you) get you very far.

Eating

Conveyor belt sushi (kaiten-zushi) at Sushiro or Hamazushi is excellent and costs ¥100–200/plate. Standing ramen bars are faster and often as good as sit-down. Convenience store food (7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson) is genuinely good — onigiri, sandwiches, hot oden. The kissaten (old-school coffee shop) serves thick toast, drip coffee, and sometimes eggs — one of the great Tokyo institutions.

What to avoid

Robot Restaurant is closed. It shut during COVID and has not reopened. Multiple operators have reused the name/concept; none are the original and none are worth the price. Skip any "Robot Restaurant experience" tours.

Don't buy a tourist day pass for the subway — the IC card charges per journey and almost always works out cheaper.

Connectivity

Pocket Wi-Fi rental (pick up at the airport) or eSIM (Airalo has Japan eSIMs from $8 for 7 days). Japan's LTE coverage is excellent everywhere including subway stations. Don't rely on hotel Wi-Fi alone — you'll want maps and translation constantly.

Behavior

Don't eat while walking — it's considered rude. Queues are orderly; join the back. Subway etiquette is strict: no talking on phone, no speakerphone, give up priority seats. Tattoos are an issue at traditional onsen (public baths) — call ahead or find a tattoo-friendly one.

Things to buy

Artisanal knives at Kappabashi (the kitchenware district near Asakusa). Specific-region Kit Kats (sakura, wasabi, sake) at the Kit Kat Chocolatory in Ginza. Vintage Levi's from the 1960s–70s — Tokyo is the best city in the world for this, particularly in Shimokitazawa and Koenji. Art books at the MUJI flagship or Tsutaya bookstore in Daikanyama.

Optional add-ons

  • Kyoto overnight (Days 5–6 alternative): 2.5 hours on the Shinkansen. Fushimi Inari (10,000 torii gates, best at dawn), Arashiyama bamboo grove, Philosopher's Path. Nishiki Market. Stay one night at a ryokan with dinner — one of the best Japan experiences available. Replaces the Nikko/Kamakura day trip with something bigger.
  • Onsen ryokan near Hakone (Day 6 alternative): 85 minutes from Shinjuku. Stay one night at a traditional inn with private onsen; Mt. Fuji visible on clear days from the lake. Hakone Open Air Museum has world-class sculpture. Return next morning.
  • Nakano Broadway: The quieter, deeper alternative to Akihabara for otaku culture — less flashy, more specialist. Old Mandarake stores full of 30-year-old figures. Add as an afternoon extension to Akihabara day.
  • Toyosu tuna auction: The most exclusive experience in this plan. About 100 visitor slots per day at 5:30am, by online lottery roughly one month in advance (apply the first week of July for August dates). If you get it, you watch multi-million-yen tuna auctioned in 10 seconds each. Most people don't get it. Try anyway.
  • Koenji: The other bohemian neighborhood (west of Shimokitazawa on the Chuo Line). More working-class, more musicians, more interesting vintage than Shimokitazawa. Add as an extension of Day 8.

Before you board — 1–2 weeks out

  • Visit Japan Web — Japan’s digital immigration + customs pre-registration. Register at services.digital.go.jp/en/visit-japan-web/ with your passport and flight details. Do 2–7 days before departure; QR codes must be accessed live on your phone at the airport — screenshots not accepted.
  • eSIM — Airalo Japan plan (~$8 for 7 days). Activate on the plane or at Narita. You’ll use data constantly for maps and Translate.
  • Suica on iPhone Wallet — load ¥5,000. Covers subway, JR, buses, and most convenience stores.
  • Notify bank and cards of Japan travel dates.
